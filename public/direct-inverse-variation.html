Direct and Inverse Variation

Direct Variation

The Question

You're paid the same rate per hour at a part-time job: 4 hours earns 20,000 so'm. Using that same rate, how much would you earn for 7 hours?

The Idea

In direct variation, y = kx for some constant k. As x gets bigger, y gets bigger by the same factor. Find k first (k = y ÷ x from what you know), then use it to find the new y.

In one line: find the rate (k = y ÷ x), then multiply by the new x.

Watch It Solved

4 hours → 20,000 so'm

find the rate: k = 20,000 ÷ 4

k = 5,000 so'm per hour

use the rate for 7 hours

y = 5,000 × 7

y = 35,000 so'm

Watch Out

A common mistake is adding instead of using the rate — going from 4 hours to 7 hours is not "+3 hours worth of the same total," you have to multiply the NEW x by the constant rate k.

Why It Matters

Direct variation is behind any "same rate" situation — hourly pay, price per kilogram, speed at a constant pace.

Inverse Variation

The Question

4 workers can paint a fence in 12 hours. Working at the same combined rate, how many hours would 6 workers take?

The Idea

In inverse variation, x × y stays constant (call it k) — as one goes up, the other goes down by the same factor. Find k = x × y from what you know, then divide by the new x to get the new y.

In one line: find k = x × y, then divide by the new x.

Watch It Solved

4 workers × 12 hours

find the constant: k = 4 × 12

k = 48

divide by the new number of workers

y = 48 ÷ 6

y = 8 hours

Watch Out

The most common mistake is treating this like direct variation and multiplying — more workers means LESS time needed, not more, so you divide by the new x, you don't multiply.

Why It Matters

Inverse variation shows up anywhere more "helpers" or "speed" means less time needed — workers finishing a job, speed vs. travel time.
