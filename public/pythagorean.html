Pythagorean Theorem

Finding the Hypotenuse

The Question

A ladder leans against a wall. Its base is 3 meters from the wall, and it reaches 4 meters up the wall. How long is the ladder?

The Idea

For a right triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c: a² + b² = c².

In one line: square both legs, add them, then take the square root.

Watch It Solved

legs 3 and 4

3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25

c = √25

c = 5

Watch Out

A common mistake is adding the legs first and then squaring — (3 + 4)² = 49, not 25. You have to square each leg separately before adding.

Why It Matters

This is how carpenters check a corner is truly square, and how GPS and screen sizes calculate straight-line distance.

Finding a Missing Leg

The Question

This time you know the ladder itself is 5 meters long, and its base is 3 meters from the wall. How high up the wall does it reach?

The Idea

If you know the hypotenuse c and one leg, you can rearrange the theorem to find the other leg: leg = √(c² − a²).

In one line: square the hypotenuse, subtract the known leg squared, then take the square root.

Watch It Solved

hypotenuse 5, known leg 3

5² − 3² = 25 − 9 = 16

missing leg = √16

= 4

Watch Out

A common mistake is adding the squares instead of subtracting — 5² + 3² = 34, which is wrong. When you're missing a leg (not the hypotenuse), you subtract, not add.

Why It Matters

This is how you'd figure out how far a ladder's base needs to sit from a wall to safely reach a certain height.
